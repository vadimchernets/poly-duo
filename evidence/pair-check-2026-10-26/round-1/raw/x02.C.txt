**Bottom line: 1/2.** Switching to door 2 wins with probability 50%, and so does staying.

**Reading chosen:** The host picks door 3 uniformly at random from the two doors you didn't pick. He could have revealed the car, but he didn't. We condition on that outcome.

**Facts (Bayes):**
- Prior: P(car at 1) = P(car at 2) = P(car at 3) = 1/3.
- Likelihood of the observed event "host opens door 3 and shows a goat":
  - Car at 1: the host opens door 3 with probability 1/2, and it is a goat. Likelihood = 1/2.
  - Car at 2: the host opens door 3 with probability 1/2, and it is a goat. Likelihood = 1/2.
  - Car at 3: the host opens door 3 with probability 1/2, but it shows the car. This contradicts the observation. Likelihood = 0.
- Joint probabilities: 1/6 for car at 1, 1/6 for car at 2, 0 for car at 3.
- Normalize: P(car at 1 | observation) = P(car at 2 | observation) = (1/6)/(1/3) = 1/2.

**Why it differs from the classic problem:** In the classic game the host knows where the car is and always opens a goat door. Under that rule the event "door 3 shows a goat" has probability 1 if the car is at 2 and 1/2 if the car is at 1. That asymmetry gives 2/3 for switching. With a random host, the likelihoods are equal. Your door-1 pick gains no extra weight, because the host's action no longer depends on where the car is.

**Conclusion:** Switching and staying are equivalent here, each winning with probability 1/2.

**Caveat:** This depends on the host choosing uniformly at random between doors 2 and 3. If he had a bias, such as preferring door 3, the answer would change. The question doesn't state one, so I assumed none.
